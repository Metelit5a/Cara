\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Skincare decisions are today driven mostly by intuition, marketing, and
influencers, while professional dermatological advice is expensive and
often inaccessible. \cara is a web application that closes part of this
gap by returning an honest, evidence-based assessment of a user's facial
skin from a single photograph, together with concrete ingredient-level
recommendations.

The final system decomposes the assessment into three complementary
classification tasks --- acne severity, skin type, and multi-label skin
conditions (pores, blackheads) --- each handled by a dedicated \effnet
convolutional neural network. The three predictions feed a
deterministic, rule-based Business Logic Processing (BLP) engine that
turns raw model outputs into actionable, explainable advice.

This report tells the honest story of how the system reached that
design. The project went through three distinct phases. \textbf{Phase 1
was pure research}: no code, no models. We scoped the problem, surveyed
competitors, mapped out what a truthful skin-analysis product would
look like, and agreed early on that recommendations would be delivered
through a deterministic rule engine rather than a learned recommender.
We also identified a set of candidate public datasets we believed
would be good enough to build the system on. \textbf{Phase 2 was the
proof of concept}: each of the four team members implemented a
different backbone architecture --- ResNet, DenseNet, Vision
Transformer, and \effnet --- trained on the ACNE04 acne-severity
dataset, in order to compare them head-to-head on a common benchmark
before committing to one. \effnet won at \(73.1\,\%\) validation
accuracy (against \(\sim 55\,\%\) for ResNet, \(\sim 62\,\%\) for
DenseNet, and \(\sim 68\,\%\) for ViT) and became the backbone of the
POC and, later, of every model we shipped. \textbf{Phase 3 was the
largest phase and the source of everything in this repository}: we
turned the single-model POC into the final multi-model application,
adding a skin-type model and a skin-conditions model, and running each
model against real user photographs for the first time. That is where
almost every hard lesson surfaced. Real-world test images exposed
catastrophic failures that a naive validation split had hidden: models
were learning dataset signatures and photographic style rather than
clinical features; a preprocessing pipeline that existed in code was
silently disconnected in production; one of the ``healthy'' training
folders turned out to be \emph{acne-level-zero} photographs with
visible blemishes; and a softmax over five skin issues was
mathematically incapable of saying ``none''. We responded by rebuilding
the datasets from the ground up, reframing skin issues as a multi-label
problem with proper negative anchors, enforcing training--inference
preprocessing parity, and adapting the model to in-the-wild
photographs by borrowing negatives from an orthogonal dataset.

The final acne and skin-type classifiers reach 72.9\,\% and 70.9\,\%
best validation accuracy on their respective test splits; the
multi-label skin-conditions classifier reaches a macro-F1 of
\textbf{0.981} after the domain-adaptation retrain. Beyond raw
accuracy, our contribution is the emphasis on \emph{truthfulness}: the
guarantee that when the system is uncertain, it says so, and that
every recommendation shown to the user can be traced to a single,
inspectable rule.

\section{The problem we set out to solve}

Skin is the largest organ in the human body, and yet the decisions
people make about their skin are among the most poorly informed
decisions in everyday life. Consumers spend billions each year on
skincare products chosen almost entirely on the basis of marketing,
celebrity endorsements, and viral trends. Dermatologists are expensive
and overbooked; general practitioners rarely have time for cosmetic
concerns; and the loudest voices online are often those with the
least qualifications.

The result is a market in which a person with mild acne might spend a
year using a moisturiser designed for dry, ageing skin, while another
person with sensitive combination skin scrubs their face with an
abrasive salicylic-acid cleanser marketed for severe cystic acne.
In both cases the person is not making a foolish choice; they simply
lack access to a truthful, personal assessment of their own skin.

\cara --- \emph{Computer-Aided Review \& Analysis} --- is our attempt to
provide a small slice of that missing information. The goal is
deliberately narrow: given a single facial photograph uploaded through
a web browser, return within a few seconds an honest report describing
the user's acne severity, skin type, and any notable skin conditions,
together with a concrete list of ingredients to look for and
ingredients to avoid.

\section{Why this problem is a good fit for deep learning}

Dermatological image classification has three properties that make
it a natural target for supervised deep learning:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The signal is visual.} Almost everything a
        dermatologist evaluates in a routine cosmetic consultation
        is visible in a well-lit photograph: comedones, papules,
        pustules, pore density, oily sheen, dry flakes,
        hyperpigmentation. There is no need to reach for extra
        modalities.
    \item \textbf{The labels are weak and noisy.} Public datasets
        come from different clinicians, different rating scales,
        and different lighting conditions. This forces us to take
        preprocessing, augmentation, and class imbalance
        seriously, rather than treating them as afterthoughts.
    \item \textbf{The sub-tasks are related but not identical.}
        Oily skin is more prone to acne; blackheads tend to
        cluster in areas with visibly enlarged pores. The
        correlations are real, but so is the fact that the tasks
        do not share a single label space. This creates a genuine
        architectural question --- one big model or several small
        ones? --- that we return to in \Cref{ch:algorithm}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Non-negotiable design principles}\label{sec:principles}

Very early in the project --- during Phase 1, before any code was
written --- we agreed on three rules that would govern every technical
decision. We refer back to them throughout the report.

\begin{description}
    \item[Truthfulness is the product.] A wrong prediction is worse
        than no prediction. If the model is not confident, the system
        must say so out loud rather than paper over the uncertainty.
    \item[No manipulated results.] We never tune a threshold to make
        an embarrassing prediction disappear. Numbers reported in this
        book are the numbers we measured.
    \item[Explainability comes from design, not post-hoc excuses.] The
        rule engine that converts model predictions into recommendations
        is deterministic and human-readable. A user (or a supervisor)
        can trace exactly why a specific recommendation was made. This
        commitment to a rule-based recommendation layer was made in
        Phase 1 and was never revisited: a learned recommender was
        never even a candidate.
\end{description}

These principles ruled out several attractive architectures:
learned recommenders, ensemble score fusion, threshold calibration on
the test set. They are also the reason the project took three phases
rather than one to reach its current shape, as \Cref{ch:data,ch:algorithm}
describe in full.

\section{Research questions}

Behind the product sits a small set of empirical questions we cared
about answering. The rest of the book returns to each of them with
evidence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item On our data budget (thousands, not millions, of images),
        which family of backbones actually generalises best ---
        classical CNNs (ResNet), densely-connected CNNs (DenseNet),
        transformer-based models (ViT), or compound-scaled CNNs
        (\effnet)? Does the answer come from the architecture itself
        or from the strength of its pretrained weights?
    \item Is it better to build one large multi-task model that
        predicts every skin aspect from a shared backbone, or to run
        several small, task-specific models in parallel? Which is
        easier to debug when something goes wrong?
    \item When the physical problem admits ``multiple'' or ``none''
        as legitimate answers, how much does moving from softmax to
        sigmoid + BCE actually change the model's behaviour on real
        inputs?
    \item Can validation-split metrics be trusted as a proxy for
        shipped quality on skin datasets, or do they systematically
        mislead? What kind of test suite would have caught the bug
        we did not catch?
    \item Can cross-task data reuse --- treating one dataset's
        images as negatives for another task --- close a
        distribution gap that would otherwise require a fresh
        labelling effort?
\end{itemize}

Each question was answered by an experiment or by a diagnosed
failure, not by intuition.

\section{Contributions}

The concrete contributions of this project are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A production-grade, end-to-end web application that runs
        three deep-learning models sequentially on a single shared
        input and returns a report within a couple of seconds on
        commodity hardware.
    \item A head-to-head comparison of four genuinely different
        backbone families (ResNet, DenseNet, Vision Transformer, and
        \effnet) on the same acne-severity benchmark, with an honest
        explanation of why \effnet won on our data budget.
    \item A multi-model architecture in which each skin sub-task is
        handled by its own dedicated \effnet classifier, feeding a
        deterministic rule-based recommendation engine that we
        committed to from the very first day of the project.
    \item A multi-label refactor of the skin-conditions problem, with
        cross-task negative-anchor mining, that raised out-of-domain
        precision without a fresh labelling effort.
    \item A three-layer regression test suite --- per-image assertions,
        population-level sanity checks, and multi-label specific
        assertions --- that catches the class of bug that ordinary
        train/val/test evaluation is structurally blind to.
    \item An honest, defensible narrative of every experiment we ran,
        including the ones that failed and why they failed.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:background}
%==============================================================================

\section{The clinical baseline}

Modern dermatology grades acne along ordinal scales such as the
Investigator's Global Assessment (IGA) or the Global Acne Grading
System (GAGS). Both scales collapse a rich visual scene into a small
number of categories (typically \emph{clear}, \emph{mild},
\emph{moderate}, \emph{severe}). Skin type is described using variants
of the classical four-way split \emph{oily}, \emph{dry}, \emph{normal},
\emph{combination}. Secondary conditions --- blackheads, dark spots,
enlarged pores, wrinkles --- are usually discussed qualitatively
rather than graded on a formal scale.

This clinical vocabulary matters because it defines the labels our
models must produce. Inventing new categories would make it impossible
for a domain expert to validate the output. We therefore aligned our
label spaces with the categories a dermatologist would recognise.

\section{Public datasets we evaluated}

Progress in dermatological deep learning has been paced by dataset
releases. The most influential public resources we considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ACNE04}~\cite{wu2019acne04}. Roughly 1{,}400 facial
        images labelled with acne severity plus per-lesion bounding
        boxes. The original paper formulates the task as label
        distribution learning; the class labels can also be used
        directly. Photographs are in-the-wild and vary widely in
        lighting and pose, which is both a curse and a blessing: they
        are noisy but they resemble real user uploads. \emph{Our
        anchor dataset for acne severity.}
    \item \textbf{Kaggle Face Skin Type / Skin Problems datasets}. A
        family of smaller, community-contributed datasets covering
        skin type and skin problem categories. Quality varies but
        they fill gaps left by the more curated academic datasets.
    \item \textbf{ISIC / HAM10000}~\cite{tschandl2018ham10000}. A
        large collection of dermoscopic images labelled with
        skin-lesion categories. Excellent for melanoma / lesion
        classification but not directly applicable to cosmetic
        assessment because the images are dermoscopic close-ups
        rather than casual photographs.
    \item \textbf{Fitzpatrick 17k}~\cite{groh2021fitzpatrick}. A
        dataset of clinical photographs annotated with Fitzpatrick
        skin type, used mostly for fairness studies. Referenced but
        not used for training.
    \item \textbf{Roboflow object-detection sets for acne and skin
        conditions.} Detection-annotated sets with bounding boxes
        around lesions or comedones. These looked useful in Phase 1
        but caused us serious problems in Phase 3, as
        \Cref{ch:data} details.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related academic work}

Four bodies of prior work directly shaped \cara. We name each one
briefly, then say what we actually took from it.

\subsection{Lesion classification on dermoscopic images}
Esteva et al.~\cite{esteva2017dermatologist} showed that a single
CNN, trained on enough clinical images, could match dermatologists
on binary melanoma classification. That paper is the reason we felt
confident that off-the-shelf CNN backbones would carry visual
dermatology signals well enough for a cosmetic (non-diagnostic)
application. It is also where our defensive stance on labelling
comes from: their pipeline succeeds because the labels are
clean, and the same lesson applies in reverse whenever we discover
that our labels are not.

\subsection{Acne severity grading}
The ACNE04 paper~\cite{wu2019acne04} is both our anchor dataset and
a design reference. Its authors frame acne grading as label-distribution
learning, arguing that categorical labels lose information because
adjacent severity buckets differ only marginally. We deliberately
chose the simpler categorical formulation despite this argument: our
downstream consumer is a rule engine that dispatches on discrete
severity buckets, and any smoother score would just be re-bucketed
before it reached the user.

\subsection{Skin-type and cosmetic assessment}
Skin-type classification is under-served in the academic literature;
most published pipelines come from industry rather than academia.
We could not find a widely-cited benchmark for the four-class
oily / dry / normal / combination task, which is why our skin-type
numbers are harder to compare to external work than our acne numbers.
This gap in the literature is one reason the Kaggle community dataset
we ended up using is the best available option rather than an
uncontroversial one.

\subsection{Multi-label medical image classification}
The Phase-3 skin-conditions refactor from softmax to sigmoid + BCE
is not something we invented: it is standard practice in multi-label
medical imaging, most famously in chest-X-ray
classification~\cite{rajpurkar2017chexnet}. Independent sigmoid heads,
binary cross-entropy with per-class positive weights, and per-class
\(F_1\) reporting all transferred cleanly to our setting; the credit
belongs to that line of work.

\section{Commercial and industrial products}

Several commercial products have appeared in the last few years that
occupy roughly the same niche as \cara. Each one influenced our design
either by example or by counter-example.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{L'Or\'eal Skin Genius / ModiFace.} A polished mobile
        experience that returns a numerical score and product
        recommendations. The recommendations are, unsurprisingly,
        drawn exclusively from the vendor's own catalogue. This
        motivated our decision to recommend \emph{ingredients} rather
        than branded products.
    \item \textbf{Perfect Corp.\ / YouCam Makeup.} Very strong face
        tracking and augmented-reality effects. Their skin-quality
        assessment is fast but has been criticised for optimistic
        outputs that appear to be tuned for engagement rather than
        accuracy. This informed our stance on truthfulness.
    \item \textbf{Curology.} A subscription tele-dermatology service
        in which real clinicians write prescriptions after reviewing
        user photographs. Not an AI product per se, but the reference
        for what a truthful assessment looks like.
    \item \textbf{Academic demos.} A number of open-source projects
        on GitHub attempt facial skin analysis with pretrained CNNs.
        Almost all of them fall into one of two failure modes: they
        return a confident label on every input, including images
        with no face; or they degrade to noise as soon as lighting
        changes. Both failures shaped the guardrails in our pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Where \cara fits in the picture}

\cara sits deliberately in the small corner of the market that
combines (i) open-source, reproducible, deep-learning components,
with (ii) a transparent, rule-based recommendation layer, and (iii)
an honest policy about uncertainty. We do not compete with L'Or\'eal
on polish, and we do not compete with Curology on medical rigour. We
compete with the countless \emph{``free online skin analysis''} demos
on the accuracy and honesty of the report they return.

\chapter{Academic Background}\label{ch:academic}
%==============================================================================

This chapter is a short refresher on the deep-learning ideas that
the rest of the book rests on, framed each time in the way they
actually show up in \cara rather than as a generic tutorial.

\section{Image classification}

At its heart, image classification is a mapping from an image to
one of a few predefined categories. In \cara those categories are
\emph{clear / mild / moderate / severe} acne, \emph{oily / dry /
normal / combination} skin, or the presence of individual skin
conditions such as pores and blackheads. Single-label heads
(``pick exactly one'') use a softmax so that the class
probabilities sum to one. Multi-label heads (``pick any subset,
possibly empty''), which we return to in
\Cref{sec:multilabel-formal}, use independent sigmoids instead.

\section{Multi-label classification}\label{sec:multilabel-formal}

Two of the three tasks in \cara are single-label (acne severity and
skin type: the categories are mutually exclusive by definition). The
third --- skin conditions --- is multi-label: a face can have
blackheads AND enlarged pores simultaneously, or neither. A softmax
over five categories, as we discovered in Phase 3, is mathematically
incapable of representing ``neither'': softmax outputs sum to one,
so \emph{something} always has to fire. Replacing the softmax with
independent sigmoids (and training with binary cross-entropy) turns
each class into its own yes / no decision, and the empty set ---
``no notable conditions'' --- becomes a legal output. This is
exactly why we adopted the multi-label formulation for the
skin-conditions model.

\section{Convolutional neural networks}

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)~\cite{lecun1998gradient} are
the workhorse of computer vision because they combine two useful
properties: they recognise a feature regardless of where in the
image it appears, and each layer looks only at nearby pixels.
Stacking convolutions builds a hierarchy of features, from edges
and textures in the shallow layers to whole objects near the top.
This progression matches what acne grading actually needs: local
texture (pimples) plus global layout (how many, how spread out).

\section{Why we chose \effnet}

\effnet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} is a compact CNN backbone that
scales depth, width, and input resolution together against a fixed
compute budget. We use \effnet-B0, the smallest family member,
because its ImageNet-pretrained weights are strong per parameter
and its footprint keeps three sequential inferences comfortably
within the response-time budget of a web request. The choice was
validated empirically in the Phase-2 bake-off (\Cref{sec:phase2-arch}),
where \effnet beat ResNet, DenseNet, and a Vision Transformer on
ACNE04. Our fine-tuned model has about \num{4.01}~million parameters
(the head is a dropout layer plus a single linear layer, sized 2
or 4 depending on the task) and roughly \num{0.39}~GFLOPs per
forward pass at \(224 \times 224\).

\section{Transfer learning}

Training a modern CNN from random weights on a few thousand images
overfits almost immediately. Transfer
learning~\cite{yosinski2014transferable} works around this by
starting from weights that were already trained on a much larger
dataset --- ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet} in our case --- and
then adapting them to the target task. In practice this is what
lets us fine-tune three different skin classifiers on datasets that
each contain only a few thousand labelled examples. Our two-stage
recipe (\Cref{sec:two-phase}) --- head-only warm-up followed by
partial unfreeze of the upper backbone blocks --- was adopted in
Phase 2 and carried into Phase 3 unchanged.

\section{Regularisation and augmentation}

Small datasets punish confident networks. We rely on three
regularisers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data augmentation.} Random resized crop, horizontal
        flips, mild rotations, colour jitter. All augmentations are
        applied to training data only, never to validation or test
        data.
    \item \textbf{Dropout} (\(p=0.3\)) in the classifier head.
    \item \textbf{Weight decay} via AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Class imbalance}\label{sec:imbalance}

Real-world skin datasets are heavily skewed. In our final acne
dataset the \emph{severe} class contains roughly one seventh as many
samples as \emph{mild}. Naive training would produce a classifier
that predicts \emph{mild} everywhere and reports respectable
accuracy.

For single-label tasks we use weighted cross-entropy with weights
derived from sklearn's balanced weighting rule:

\begin{equation}
    w_k = \frac{N}{K \cdot n_k}
    \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}

where \(N\) is the total number of training samples, \(K\) the number
of classes, and \(n_k\) the number of samples in class \(k\). The
intuition is simple: a class with few samples gets a large weight so
that each of its examples counts for more in the loss, and a class
with many samples gets a small weight so that it does not dominate.
Multiplying the standard cross-entropy loss by \(w_k\) whenever the
true class is \(k\) is enough to bring rare classes back into the
gradient. The weights we actually used are listed in \Cref{ch:data}.

For multi-label tasks (\Cref{sec:multilabel-formal}) we use a
per-class positive weight inside BCE, computed as the ratio of
negative to positive samples for that class, clipped to
\([0.1, 10]\) to keep the loss numerically stable.

\section{Optimisation}

We use AdamW with an initial learning rate of \(10^{-3}\) for the
classifier head and \(10^{-4}\) for the unfrozen backbone. Learning
rate scheduling is cosine annealing with a short linear warm-up.
Early stopping is triggered by validation loss (or, for the
multi-label model, validation macro-\(F_1\)), not by training loss.

\section{Rule-based systems and BLP}

Business Logic Processing (BLP) is our internal name for the
deterministic rule engine that converts model outputs into
user-facing advice. It is closer in spirit to a decision table than
to a formal rule system such as Datalog: every recommendation is
produced by evaluating a hand-written rule against the model's
structured prediction, and any recommendation shown to the user can
be traced to a single entry in a JSON file. This was a Phase-1
commitment that never wavered. \Cref{sec:blp-engine} describes the
engine we ultimately built.

\section{Metrics beyond accuracy}\label{sec:metrics}

We report accuracy for single-label tasks and macro-\(F_1\) for the
multi-label task because those are the metrics a non-expert reader
expects. Internally we track:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Macro-averaged \(F_1\).} The unweighted mean of the
        per-class \(F_1\) scores. Insensitive to class imbalance.
    \item \textbf{Balanced accuracy.} The unweighted mean of
        per-class recall. Comparable to accuracy on balanced data
        but more honest on imbalanced data.
    \item \textbf{Confusion matrix.} The full per-class breakdown.
        Any conversation about where a model is failing starts here.
    \item \textbf{Real-world regression pass rate.} The percentage of
        our hand-labelled in-the-wild photographs on which the model
        predicts correctly. This turned out to be a much better
        proxy for shipped quality than any validation-split number.
\end{itemize}

The final bullet is a lesson from Phase 3: no matter how carefully
constructed, a validation split of a training set only proves that
the model can memorise its own distribution. \Cref{sec:regression}
describes the regression suite we built to measure the real thing.

%==============================================================================
\chapter{The Data}\label{ch:data}
%==============================================================================

Data is the single largest determinant of model quality in this
project, and by a wide margin. This chapter documents where the data
came from, what we discovered when we looked closely, and how the
datasets evolved across the three phases. The evolution is important
because the \emph{same} model trained on the \emph{same} architecture
gave completely different real-world behaviour before and after each
data intervention.

\section{Phase 1: mapping the data landscape (no code yet)}

Phase 1 was a research phase. We did not train, evaluate, or even
implement anything during Phase 1. What we did do was decide which
skin aspects the product would cover and go looking for public
datasets that we \emph{believed} could support each of them. Our
initial plan was ambitious: five separate models, one for each of
acne severity, skin type, blackheads, pores, and dark spots. For
each aspect we shortlisted the best-looking candidate we could find
in the free / open-source world. On paper the shortlist looked
reasonable. We also settled, during Phase 1, on two decisions that
never changed later:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Recommendations would be produced by a deterministic
        rule engine (BLP), not by a learned recommender. This was
        a Phase-1 commitment that we never revisited.
    \item We leaned toward the idea that each skin aspect would
        eventually be handled by its own dedicated model, rather
        than by one large multi-task network. The reasoning was
        simple: if a single monolithic model misbehaves on a real
        user photograph, it is very hard to say which sub-problem
        is at fault. But since Phase 1 was research-only and no
        model had actually been trained yet, this remained an
        \emph{open question} rather than a committed decision;
        we knew we would have to answer it for real once we
        started scaling up. The final decision was made in
        Phase 3 (\Cref{sec:phase3-scale-up}).
\end{itemize}

The important caveat is that in Phase 1 the datasets were only
\emph{candidates on disk}. We had not yet trained a single model on
them and therefore had no way to see the quality problems that
would later surface in Phase 3. Phase 1 ended with a plan, a
recommendation strategy, a shortlist of datasets, and no code.

\section{Phase 2: implementing a POC on ACNE04 only}

Phase 2 was the proof of concept. We deliberately did \emph{not}
attempt to build all the models at once. Instead, we picked one
dataset --- the acne-severity dataset ACNE04~\cite{wu2019acne04} ---
and used it as a common benchmark to decide which backbone
architecture our whole system would be built on
(\Cref{sec:phase2-arch}). ACNE04 was the right choice for that job:
it is the cleanest and best-documented of the datasets we had
shortlisted, has four ordinal severity classes, and its
in-the-wild photographs resemble the kind of image a real user
would upload.

Because Phase 2 only touched ACNE04, none of the dataset-quality
problems described later in this chapter were visible yet. Phase 2
ended with a working end-to-end POC: an image upload flow, an
\effnet acne-severity classifier, a first version of the BLP rule
engine covering the acne branch, and a simple report page. It
proved that the shape of the product was viable; it did not yet
prove that our other candidate datasets would hold up.

\section{Phase 3: expanding to multiple datasets, and everything that broke}

Phase 3 turned the POC into the final product. That meant taking
the working \effnet backbone and instantiating it three times ---
once for acne severity, once for skin type, once for the group of
secondary skin issues --- each with its own dataset, its own
training run, and its own head. This is also the phase where the
codebase in front of you was actually written; almost none of the
Phase-2 code survives verbatim into the current repository.

The moment we started training the second and third models on the
non-ACNE04 datasets that Phase 1 had shortlisted, cracks appeared.
Some were in the data itself, some in the preprocessing pipeline,
some in the loss function. \Cref{ch:algorithm} covers the pipeline
and loss fixes; the rest of this chapter documents what we found
when we looked at the training folders themselves.

Two Phase-1 dataset choices, in particular, turned out to be
catastrophic once real-world testing began:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item An early candidate for the acne task was an
        \textbf{object-detection} dataset --- photographs annotated
        with bounding boxes around individual pimples. The idea was
        to run an object detector, count boxes, and derive severity
        from the count. In practice this would have meant almost no
        ``clear'' examples (nobody annotates healthy faces with zero
        boxes), and the signal any eventual classifier would learn
        would have been ``how much stuff got detected'', not ``what
        is the clinical severity''. We dropped this idea in Phase 3
        in favour of using ACNE04 as a pure classification dataset
        (labels only, bounding boxes discarded).
    \item The ``healthy'' folder inside the initial multi-issue skin
        dataset consisted of images with the filename prefix
        \texttt{levle0\_*.jpg} --- level-0 acne from a different
        dataset. Level 0 is \emph{not} the same as clean skin.
        Those photographs actually contain visible pimples and
        blackheads, just less severely. Trained as a ``healthy''
        class it taught the model exactly the wrong concept. This
        was invisible in Phase 1 (we never trained on it) and would
        have been invisible in Phase 2 as well (we only touched
        ACNE04). It surfaced the moment we ran the newly trained
        skin-issues model on a real user selfie in Phase 3.
\end{enumerate}

The lesson from these two examples is one we return to throughout
the book: a validation-split accuracy number computed on the same
folder the model was trained on cannot detect a mislabelled folder.
The model happily memorises whatever labels it is given. Only real
photographs from outside the training distribution can expose the
gap.

\section{The Phase-3 dataset audit}

Phase 3 began in earnest with a single failing photograph: a
well-lit adult selfie of a clean face that came back as \emph{severe
acne, oily skin, wrinkles detected}. Every part of that answer was
wrong. Chasing the cause down took us through the data, the
preprocessing pipeline, and the loss functions. The pipeline and
loss fixes are covered in \Cref{ch:algorithm}; this section covers
what we found when we looked at the training folders themselves.

\subsection{The ``healthy'' folder was not healthy}

The initial multi-issue skin-issues dataset that we picked up in
Phase 3 (from the Phase-1 shortlist) contained a \texttt{healthy/}
subfolder whose files were named \texttt{levle0\_*.jpg}. Those
images had originally been drawn from an external acne-severity
level-0 collection. Level 0 is the lowest acne grade, but it is
not the absence of blemishes: inspection showed visible pimples,
blackheads, and mild dark spots in most of the files. Trained on
this folder as an example of ``healthy'' skin, the model learned
the wrong concept and never predicted \texttt{healthy} on real
clean-skin selfies. Our regression suite later caught this
as \(0/9\) real photos classified as healthy, which matched the
diagnosis exactly.

\subsection{The ``wrinkles'' folder was elderly stock photography}

The \texttt{wrinkles/} folder in the same initial multi-issue
dataset came from a Roboflow crop of stock photographs of elderly
faces. The images share a very specific photographic style: studio
lighting, posed smile, older subjects, high-quality DSLR. What the
classifier learned was ``photograph of an older-looking person'',
not ``fine lines on skin'', and it accordingly fired
\texttt{wrinkles} on any mid-adult selfie.

Both folder problems share the same underlying failure: the class
label describes a superficial property of the image rather than
the feature the label claims to identify. Neither was visible
under train-split evaluation because the train and validation
splits share the same photographic style; both were visible the
moment a real user upload arrived. This is one of the reasons the
initial Phase-3 skin-issues model performed so much worse on real
photographs than its validation number suggested, and it is why
we now insist on spot-checking every folder before it is accepted
into training.

\subsection{Softmax cannot say ``none''}

Even if the two folder issues had been fixed in place, the
skin-issues model would still have insisted on picking exactly one
of \{healthy, blackheads, dark spots, pores, wrinkles\} for every
input. Real skin does not work like that. A face can have
blackheads and pores simultaneously, or neither. Under a softmax
head the probabilities sum to one, and there is no output that
means ``none of the above''; the model is mathematically forced
to pick a class. The architectural response is described in
\Cref{sec:multi-label-refactor}.

The first Phase-3 retrain used the 483 curated clean-skin faces from

conditions'' on iPhone selfies. The problem was that the negatives
shared one very specific photographic style (clinical ACNE04
portraits) while the positives (pores, blackheads) were arbitrary
Roboflow internet crops. The model was learning \emph{style}, not
\emph{condition}. The next chapter (\Cref{sec:domain-adaptation})
describes the retrain that fixed this.

\subsection{BCE positive weights for skin conditions}

We compute per-class positive weights automatically from the actual
training split. Concretely:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \(p_{\text{pores}} = n^{\text{neg}}_{\text{pores}} /
        n^{\text{pos}}_{\text{pores}} \approx 2.39\).
    \item \(p_{\text{blackheads}} = n^{\text{neg}}_{\text{blackheads}} /
        n^{\text{pos}}_{\text{blackheads}} \approx 1.81\).
\end{itemize}

Both are within the safety clip range \([0.1, 10]\) so we do not
have to worry about numerical instability, and both are recomputed
whenever the dataset changes.

\section{Splits}

For datasets we split ourselves (\texttt{acne\_severity} and
\texttt{skin\_conditions}) we use a stratified random 70 / 15 / 15
split with a fixed seed, so the exact split is the same across
training runs and a validation curve from one week can be compared
against one from the next. The skin-type dataset is used as
published, with the authors' existing train / valid / test folders
respected.

\section{Cleaning}

The community datasets are noisy. During Phase 3 we spent
substantial time in manual triage. The main categories of removed
samples were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Duplicate or near-duplicate images.
    \item Images with heavy makeup or filters that obviously masked
        the underlying skin condition.
    \item Non-facial images that had slipped into face-labelled
        folders.
    \item Extremely low-resolution images (short side smaller than
        \(200\) pixels).
    \item Mislabelled samples where three annotators from the team
        disagreed with the dataset's label.
\end{itemize}

\section{Augmentation}

Training-time augmentations, in order:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Random resized crop with scale in \([0.85, 1.0]\).
    \item Random horizontal flip with probability \(0.5\).
    \item Random rotation up to \(\pm 12^\circ\).
    \item Colour jitter with brightness, contrast, and saturation
        each in \([\,{-}0.15, +0.15\,]\).
    \item ImageNet-normalisation to tensor.
\end{enumerate}

Validation and test transforms consist only of a resize, a centre
crop to \(224 \times 224\), and ImageNet normalisation.

\begin{observation}
Early in Phase 2 we tried more aggressive augmentations
(RandAugment, random erasing, strong colour jitter). All of them
hurt skin-type accuracy in particular, because they alter the exact
cues (colour, texture) the model relies on. This is one of the
pitfalls that is easy to miss when reusing recipes from the ImageNet
literature. The final augmentation stack is deliberately gentle.
\end{observation}

This is the main technical chapter of the book. It walks through the
final pipeline first, then unpacks the decisions that led to it,
including the ones that failed. The chapter is structured around the

\section{The final pipeline, end to end}\label{sec:final-pipeline}

\Cref{fig:pipeline-final} is the state of the algorithm at the point
of submission. Everything else in this chapter explains why each
box is where it is.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{tikz/pipeline-final.pdf}
    \caption{End-to-end runtime pipeline used by \cara at
    submission time. The three \effnet models run sequentially on
    a shared preprocessed tensor; the BLP engine composes their
    outputs deterministically. Each of the arrows in the diagram
    was, at one point in Phase 3, either wrong or missing.}
    \label{fig:pipeline-final}
\end{figure}

Concretely, at inference time we execute:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Decode the uploaded image bytes with PIL, apply
        \texttt{ImageOps.exif\_transpose} so phone-orientation
        metadata is respected.
    \item Detect a face with an OpenCV Haar cascade. If the upright
        detection fails, retry the detector at
        \(\pm 15^\circ, \pm 30^\circ, \pm 45^\circ, \pm 90^\circ,
        180^\circ\), rotate the underlying image to whichever angle
        succeeded, and crop tightly.
    \item Resize to \(224 \times 224\), apply ImageNet normalisation,
        wrap in a \pytorch tensor.
    \item Run the three \effnet models on the same tensor.
        Acne severity and skin type produce softmax distributions
        over four classes each; the multi-label skin-conditions
        model produces two independent sigmoid probabilities. Any
        sigmoid output above the (per-class-configured) threshold
        of 0.5 becomes a ``finding''.
    \item Feed the structured predictions into the BLP engine, which
        walks its JSON rules and produces a deduplicated list of
        ingredient recommendations, avoidances, and explanations.
    \item Serialise to JSON and persist under
        \texttt{storage/reports/\{report\_id\}.json}.
\end{enumerate}

Every stage in this list has an entry in the rest of this chapter.

\section{Why \effnet, and why three of them}

Choosing the backbone was an early decision that survived all three
project phases. \effnet was picked at the end of Phase 2 through the
head-to-head bake-off in \Cref{sec:phase2-arch}, where it beat
ResNet, DenseNet, and a Vision Transformer on the same ACNE04
benchmark. Beyond the raw accuracy number, its pretrained ImageNet
weights are extremely strong per parameter and its smallest family
member (\effnet-B0) is compact enough to run three sequential
inferences on modest hardware inside our target latency, which
matters for a web application.

The follow-up question --- ``one big model or three small ones?''
--- had been an open leaning since Phase 1 rather than a settled
decision, and Phase 3 was where we finally had to answer it in code
(\Cref{sec:phase3-scale-up}). The short version is that a single
multi-task \effnet cannot balance three tasks whose datasets have
different sizes, different label noise, and different natural class
priors, and that a shared backbone makes it very hard to attribute a
real-world failure to a specific sub-task. Three independent
\effnet models won cleanly.

\section{Two-stage transfer learning}\label{sec:two-phase}

Each of the three models is trained with the following two-stage
recipe, taken directly from
\texttt{model\_service/training/train.py}. It is the same recipe
that \effnet used to win the Phase-2 bake-off (\Cref{sec:phase2-arch});
we adopted it from the very beginning and never departed from it.
Note that the two ``stages'' below refer to \emph{training stages}
of a single model, not to the three project phases discussed
elsewhere in the book.

\begin{description}
    \item[Stage 1 --- head only.] The entire \effnet backbone is
        frozen. Only the newly-initialised classifier head is
        trained, with learning rate \(10^{-3}\). This lets the head
        learn to project ImageNet features onto the new label space
        without disturbing them.
    \item[Stage 2 --- full fine-tune.] The upper blocks of the
        backbone (blocks 6--8, about 3.16 million of the 4.01 million
        parameters, i.e.\ 78.8\,\%) are unfrozen and trained with
        learning rate \(10^{-4}\); the head keeps its
        \(10^{-3}\).
\end{description}

Empirically, Stage 1 alone gets us most of the way to the final
accuracy on all three tasks; Stage 2 typically adds another
6--10 percentage points on the single-label tasks and pushes
macro-\(F_1\) from around \(0.94\) to above \(0.98\) on the
multi-label task.

for the acne severity task, taken from

plateaus around \(0.65\); Stage 2 continues from a similar starting
point and lifts the best validation accuracy to \(0.729\).

\section{Phase 2: comparing four backbone architectures on ACNE04}\label{sec:phase2-arch}

Phase 2 was the phase in which we picked the backbone that survives
to the final system. The setup was deliberate: we wanted the choice
to be data-driven, so we ran a head-to-head bake-off on a single
common benchmark --- the acne-severity dataset ACNE04 --- and each
of the four team members took ownership of one architecture from a
genuinely different family of computer-vision models. Each
candidate was implemented in a separate side project (not visible
in this repository); the final numbers were compared side by side
at the end of Phase 2.

Every candidate used the same acne dataset (ACNE04, treated as a
four-class classification problem), the same train / validation /
test split, and the same evaluation protocol. Every candidate was
initialised from ImageNet-pretrained weights and fine-tuned on
ACNE04 --- otherwise we would have been measuring the value of
pretraining rather than the value of the architecture.

Two design decisions that later became load-bearing were already in
place before the bake-off began. First, from Phase 1 the
recommendation layer was going to be a deterministic rule engine
(BLP), not a learned fusion --- so nothing in this comparison was
about how model outputs should be combined into recommendations,
only about which model gives the best per-task predictions. Second,
we had already decided that in the eventual multi-model system each
skin aspect would have its own dedicated backbone. The purpose of
Phase 2 was to answer ``which backbone family?'', not ``one model or
many?''.

\subsection{Candidate 1 --- ResNet (classical residual CNN)}

ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} is the classical residual CNN and the
backbone that has been the default baseline for image classification
for a decade. Residual connections let gradients flow directly
through skip paths, which is why deep ResNets train stably.

\emph{Result on ACNE04: \(\sim 55\,\%\) validation accuracy.} On a
few thousand mildly-labelled facial photographs this was the weakest
of the four candidates. The most likely explanation is that ResNet
was designed and pretrained for very large-scale classification
tasks (millions of ImageNet images); its raw feature extractor is
good, but it does not carry the strong per-task inductive biases of
the newer architectures, and on our dataset scale the extra capacity
turns into an overfitting liability rather than an accuracy boost.

\subsection{Candidate 2 --- DenseNet (dense feature reuse)}

DenseNet~\cite{huang2017densely} connects every layer to every
later layer in the same block, so each layer receives as input the
concatenated feature maps of all its predecessors. In principle
this improves gradient flow and encourages the network to reuse
low-level features rather than relearn them at every depth --- a
property that intuitively helps on small datasets, where relearning
features is exactly what you cannot afford.

\emph{Result on ACNE04: \(\sim 62\,\%\) validation accuracy.} A
clear step up from ResNet. Dense feature reuse did seem to help
extract more from a limited number of ACNE04 photographs, but the
computational cost of concatenating many feature maps at every
depth also made this the slowest of the CNN candidates during
training, which mattered given the modest compute we had
available.

\subsection{Candidate 3 --- Vision Transformer (ViT)}

The Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} discards
convolutions entirely and treats an image as a sequence of small
patches, processed by self-attention. In theory this gives the
model a much wider effective receptive field --- every patch can
attend to every other patch from layer one --- which we suspected
might help on acne, since severity depends both on individual
lesions and on how many of them are spread across the face.

\emph{Result on ACNE04: \(\sim 68\,\%\) validation accuracy.} ViT
was the second-strongest candidate and the one that made the
choice interesting rather than obvious. The global self-attention
did seem to help capture the ``how spread out is the acne'' cue
that is important for the moderate-vs-severe boundary. The
practical downside is that transformer backbones are known to need
more data and more compute than CNNs to hit their best numbers,
and on ACNE04 --- with a few hundred images per class and a laptop
CPU budget --- ViT was still visibly under-trained at the point
where the compound-scaled EfficientNet had already converged.

\subsection{Candidate 4 --- \effnet (winner)}

\effnet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} is a compound-scaled CNN that
jointly balances depth, width, and input resolution against a
fixed compute budget. Its ImageNet weights are extremely strong
per parameter, and its small model in the family (\effnet-B0)
fits comfortably in our compute budget.

\emph{Result on ACNE04: \(\mathbf{73.1\,\%}\) validation accuracy;
\(\mathbf{69.5\,\%}\) accuracy on the held-out test set.} Best of
the four, by a clear margin. The two-stage training recipe we
committed to from the beginning (\Cref{sec:two-phase}) worked
particularly well here: with the backbone frozen for the first ten
epochs the head alone already reached \(56.5\,\%\) validation
accuracy, and unfreezing the upper layers for another five epochs
lifted validation accuracy by another sixteen or so points to
\(73.1\,\%\). No other candidate showed that kind of improvement
from fine-tuning in the same compute envelope.

The Phase-2 conclusion was straightforward: while ViT proved
itself to be a very strong architecture and took second place,
\effnet won cleanly. The most credible explanation is not that
\effnet is a fundamentally smarter architecture than a Vision
Transformer, but that its pretrained weights are so well tuned
that on a dataset of our size fine-tuning goes further faster.
Compound scaling and pretraining together gave us the best
accuracy-per-parameter and the best accuracy-per-training-hour on
the hardware we actually had, and that is the choice we shipped.

At the end of Phase 2 we shipped a first proof of concept built on

\section{Phase 3: from a single-model POC to a multi-model application}\label{sec:phase3-scale-up}

Phase 3 turned the POC into the final product. Because the POC only
covered acne severity, the very first Phase-3 task was to
instantiate the winning \effnet backbone twice more --- once for
skin type, once for a group of secondary skin issues --- each with
its own dataset and its own training run. This is the moment where
the Phase-1 shortlist of datasets stopped being a plan and started
being training material, and this is the moment where every
weakness in that shortlist began to show.

The multi-model layout also required a decision that Phase 2 had
not needed to make. Given three per-task classifiers, how should
their outputs be combined into a single report and a single
recommendation list? We considered the following options:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One big multi-task \effnet with three heads.} A
        single shared backbone with three classification heads and
        a weighted-sum loss. \emph{Rejected} because the three
        datasets differ in size and label noise, the three heads
        converge at different rates, and a shared backbone gets
        pulled around by whichever head has the loudest gradient
        signal at any moment --- exactly the kind of coupling that
        makes it very hard to figure out which sub-problem is
        misbehaving on a real user photograph.
    \item \textbf{Three independent classifiers, weighted-vote
        fusion.} Three independent \effnet classifiers, one per
        task; a small set of per-model score thresholds combined
        by a weighted vote to produce recommendations.
        \emph{Rejected} because a scalar vote is not expressive
        enough for compound rules of the form ``if acne is
        moderate \emph{and} skin type is oily, recommend a BHA
        and warn against occlusives''. To recover that
        expressiveness we would have had to re-implement a rule
        engine underneath the vote --- worse than starting from a
        rule engine directly.
    \item \textbf{Three independent classifiers, learned MLP
        fusion.} Feed the concatenated softmax vectors into a
        small MLP whose output is the recommendation set.
        \emph{Rejected} because the recommendation vocabulary is
        a labelled set (which ingredients apply to which
        combination of predictions), and building ground truth
        for that vocabulary requires a dermatologist. Without such
        a signal the MLP has nothing meaningful to train against,
        and any labels we could generate ourselves would just be
        a reflection of the rules we would have written by hand
        anyway --- with the added cost that the MLP would then
        obscure the trace from prediction to recommendation,
        breaking our Phase-1 explainability commitment.
    \item \textbf{Three independent classifiers, rule-based BLP.}
        Three dedicated \effnet classifiers plus a deterministic
        rule engine that walks a JSON rules file and unions all
        matching recommendations. Every recommendation shown to
        the user can be traced to a single JSON entry.
        \emph{Selected.} The rule engine was the recommendation
        strategy we had committed to since Phase 1; the
        independent-classifier design was the choice that gave us
        the debuggability we wanted.
\end{itemize}

The selection here is not a new decision --- it is the
consequence of the Phase-1 rule-based commitment plus the
observation that a shared multi-task backbone would have made
Phase-3 debugging materially harder. Everything that follows in
this chapter is the story of what happened once that
architecture met real user photographs.

\section{Phase 3, part 1: real-world failures}\label{sec:phase3-failures}

Real-world testing began with an ordinary event: one of us
uploaded a personal selfie and got back a report that read
\emph{severe acne, oily skin, wrinkles detected}. Every part of
that answer was wrong. The selfie was of a clean adult face with
no acne, no oiliness, and no visible wrinkles.

That single photograph triggered an investigation that surfaced
three separate defects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A frontend field mismatch that displayed ``Low Confidence''
        on every report regardless of the model's actual confidence,
        which hid the real problem for weeks.
    \item A preprocessing mismatch: the models were trained on
        tight face crops but were being fed the full uploaded image
        at inference time. See \Cref{sec:preprocessing}.
    \item An architectural mismatch: the skin-issues model was a
        softmax over five classes and was mathematically forced to
        pick one, so it always hallucinated something on clean
        faces. See \Cref{sec:multi-label-refactor}.
\end{itemize}

The three defects compounded, and their interaction was itself
instructive. Once the frontend rendering was fixed we could see
that the acne model was returning very low confidence on the
photograph, which should have hidden the label under our
confidence gate; the broken confidence field had been masking
that. Once confidence was correctly rendered we could see that
the preprocessing mismatch was producing spurious high-confidence
predictions on in-the-wild inputs. And once preprocessing was
fixed we could see that the skin-issues model still hallucinated
at least one finding on every clean face, because it was
structurally incapable of saying ``none''. Each fix uncovered the
next.

\section{Preprocessing parity}\label{sec:preprocessing}

Preprocessing is where a large fraction of our Phase-3 engineering
effort went, and probably the largest single contributor to
real-world accuracy after data quality.

\subsection{Face detection and cropping}
Uploaded images vary wildly. Some are tight selfies; some are group
photos; some are landscape shots with a face in the corner. Feeding
the raw image to the model wastes most of the receptive field on
background pixels and, worse, invites the model to key on
background cues (clothing, walls, hair) that correlated with the
dataset label distribution during training. We therefore detect
the face first and crop tightly, matching the training distribution
that the models had already learned.

We considered three detectors: OpenCV Haar cascade, MediaPipe Face
Detection, and MTCNN. Haar is by far the fastest and shipped with
OpenCV, but only detects upright frontal faces. MediaPipe is
robust across poses but adds a moderate dependency. MTCNN is the
most accurate but noticeably slow on CPU. Given our latency budget
and the fact that our target user submits a selfie, we chose Haar
with a rotation-retry fallback (see below) rather than adding a
new dependency.

\subsection{The dead-code discovery}
The early Phase-3 codebase already contained a full face-detection
preprocessor in \texttt{model\_service/preprocessing/pipeline.py}.
Its docstring promised: \emph{``Shared by both inference AND
training so the distributions match.''} It was, in fact, never
imported by the runtime path. The training scripts called it; the
analysis service did not. Every real user upload was going
straight into the model as a full uncropped image.

The fix was a small wiring change in the analysis service:
instantiate the pipeline once, pass every uploaded image through
it before inference, and require a face to be detected before the
models are consulted:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,caption={Wiring the preprocessing pipeline into the analysis service.}]
class AnalysisService:
    def __init__(self, orchestrator: InferenceOrchestrator, ...):
        self._orchestrator = orchestrator
        self._pipeline = PreprocessingPipeline(
            require_face=True,   # NEW in Phase 3
            apply_mask=False,
        )

    def analyze(self, image_bytes: bytes) -> AnalysisReport:
        pre, tensor = self._pipeline.process(image_bytes)
        if not pre.success:
            return no_face_report(pre.status_message)
        predictions = self._orchestrator.predict_all(tensor)
        return self._build_report(predictions)
\end{lstlisting}

Concrete impact: on our nine hand-labelled real-world photographs,
acne accuracy went from 3/9 to 7/9 correct and false-\emph{severe}
alarms dropped from 2/9 to 1/9 --- with no model retraining. The
model was fine; it just needed to see the same input it had trained
on.

\subsection{EXIF orientation and rotation retry}\label{sec:rotation}
After wiring the pipeline in we hit a second bug on a real photograph
taken lying down (head tilted about \(15^\circ\) on a pillow, iPhone
JPEG with an EXIF orientation tag). Face detection failed with
``No face detected'' even though the photograph clearly showed a
face. Two root causes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{cv2.imdecode} ignores EXIF orientation, so the
        iPhone photograph loaded sideways into the array.
    \item Even after we corrected orientation, the Haar cascade only
        detects upright frontal faces, so a mildly tilted head fails
        the detector.
\end{enumerate}

Both fixes live in \texttt{pipeline.py}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Decode with PIL and apply
        \texttt{ImageOps.exif\_transpose} before handing off to
        OpenCV. This respects the phone's orientation tag.
    \item After the upright detection attempt fails, retry the
        detector on rotated copies of the image at
        \(\pm 15^\circ, \pm 30^\circ, \pm 45^\circ, \pm 90^\circ,
        180^\circ\). When one succeeds we crop from the rotated
        image so that the resulting face is upright, matching the
        training distribution.
\end{itemize}

A separate test in the regression suite --- ``at least 80\,\% of
real-world photographs must produce a face detection'' --- guards
against this regression at CI time.

\section{Phase 3, part 2: refactoring skin issues to multi-label}\label{sec:multi-label-refactor}

After preprocessing parity was restored the acne and skin-type
models behaved as they should on real photographs, but the
skin-issues model continued to hallucinate wrinkles or dark spots
on clean faces. The root cause was the softmax head we described in
\Cref{sec:multilabel-formal}: it was mathematically forced to pick
exactly one of \{healthy, blackheads, dark spots, pores, wrinkles\}
and could not say ``none''.

The refactor changed six things at once:

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{The single-label to multi-label refactor.}
    \label{tab:multi-label-refactor}
    \small
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lXX}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Aspect} & \textbf{Before (single-label)} & \textbf{After (multi-label)} \\
        \midrule
        Loss function      & CrossEntropyLoss              & BCEWithLogitsLoss \\
        Output shape       & Softmax (sum to 1)            & Sigmoid per class (independent) \\
        Prediction         & Argmax                        & Threshold each class independently \\
        Label format       & Single integer                & Multi-hot vector \([1,0,1]\) \\
        ``None'' possible? & No (forced to pick)           & \textbf{Yes} (all sigmoids below threshold) \\
        Reported metric    & Accuracy                      & Per-class \(F_1\), macro-\(F_1\) \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

At the same time we shrank the label space from five classes to
two: only \emph{pores} and \emph{blackheads}. The reasons for
dropping the other three are in \Cref{tab:dropped-classes}; the
short version is that \emph{healthy} is now implicit under
multi-label (empty set = no notable conditions) and the other two
were dataset-quality casualties.

\subsection{Downstream code changes}

Multi-label is not just a training decision; it changes the shape
of the data at every boundary. The downstream edits are worth
listing because they were the majority of the Phase-3 diff:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{shared/schemas.py}: added
        \texttt{SkinCondition} enum,
        \texttt{SkinConditionFinding},
        \texttt{MultiLabelPrediction}. Kept the deprecated
        \texttt{SkinIssue} enum so historical reports on disk still
        deserialise.
    \item \texttt{model\_service/training/train.py}: added a
        \texttt{multi\_label=True} code path with
        \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}, per-class \(F_1\) evaluation,
        threshold-based prediction, and a new
        \texttt{MultiLabelImageFolderDataset} that reads multi-hot
        labels from the three-folder layout.
    \item \texttt{model\_service/inference/orchestrator.py}:
        \texttt{predict()} branches to
        \texttt{\_predict\_multi\_label()} for the skin-conditions
        model, applies the threshold, and sorts findings by
        confidence.
    \item \texttt{backend/blp/engine.py}: iterates the findings list,
        OR-combines the recommendation sets from the matching rules,
        deduplicates by ingredient.
    \item \texttt{backend/services/analysis\_service.py}: the
        confidence gate handles both single-label
        (\(\text{conf} \ge \text{threshold}\)) and multi-label
        (\(|\text{findings}| > 0\)) prediction shapes.
    \item \texttt{frontend/src/pages/ResultsPage.js}: renders a list
        of findings, or a ``no notable conditions'' chip when the
        list is empty.
    \item Tests: rewrote \texttt{test\_blp.py} around multi-label
        semantics and added multi-label-specific assertions to
        \texttt{test\_real\_world\_regression.py}.
\end{itemize}

At the end of the refactor we retrained the skin-conditions model
from scratch on the ACNE04-only negatives (retrain \#1). Its
held-out macro-\(F_1\) hit \(0.996\). We were briefly optimistic.

\section{Phase 3, part 3: the domain-adaptation retrain}\label{sec:domain-adaptation}

The regression suite told a different story. On our set of nine
stock-photo test images plus two iPhone selfies, retrain \#1
misfired \texttt{blackheads=0.98--1.00} on 7 of 11 photographs, even
though the user's own iPhone selfies came back correctly with
\texttt{blackheads=0.29} (below threshold).

This is the same class of failure as the preprocessing bug earlier,
just displaced. The training-set macro-\(F_1\) of \(0.996\) proved
that the model could discriminate its own training distribution
almost perfectly. It said nothing about how the model behaved when
the input distribution shifted, because the test split shares the
same photographic style as the training set.

Concretely, our positives (\texttt{pores}, \texttt{blackheads})
were arbitrary Roboflow crops from the internet, while our
negatives (all 483 of them, from \texttt{acne\_severity/clear/})
came from one very specific photographic style: clinical ACNE04
portraits. The model discovered that ``ACNE04-style photograph''
was an excellent proxy for ``no condition'' and locked onto it.
Everything that did not look ACNE04-clinical --- including most
stock photographs and, in general, real user selfies --- got
flagged as positive for blackheads.

\subsection{The creative fix: cross-task negative mining}

Sourcing a fresh set of hand-labelled clean-skin photographs would
have taken days and a lot of energy. Instead, we noticed that we
already had \(\sim 4{,}000\) additional in-the-wild face photographs
sitting in the skin-type training data. Skin type (oily / dry /
normal / combination) is orthogonal to the presence of pores and
blackheads. Perhaps we could reuse those photographs as extra
negative anchors for the skin-conditions model.

Naive reuse was dangerous. Some skin-type photographs undeniably
contain real mild pores or blackheads --- adding them as
\texttt{[0,0]} labels would introduce label noise. And dumping all
4{,}000 into the negatives folder would swamp the two positive
classes and skew the class balance.

We compromised by quota-sampling a ``cleanest-leaning'' subset
weighted toward classes least likely to correlate visually with
pores or blackheads:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
normal      & 500 \\
dry         & 500 \\
combination & 248 (all available in \texttt{train/}) \\
oily        & 200 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & 1\,448 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

We prefixed every added file with \texttt{skintype\_<class>\_} so
that (i) the additions are easy to identify on disk, (ii) they are
easy to remove if we regret them later, and (iii) an auditor can
verify what fraction of the negatives folder came from
which source.

The final negatives folder ended up with 1{,}931 images
(\(483 + 1{,}448\)), the pores folder with 1{,}632, and the
blackheads folder with 1{,}970 --- a manageable class ratio.

\subsection{The retrain and the honest numbers}

That drop is not a regression. The first model was overfitting to
a narrow photographic style; the second is exposed to a much
wider distribution, so its training-set macro-\(F_1\) is lower but
its predictions on real user photographs are much better
calibrated. The trade has a name: graceful degradation of the
in-distribution metric in exchange for out-of-distribution
generalisation.

The regression-suite numbers make the trade explicit. On the same
set of 11 real-world photographs:

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering

    model, before and after the domain-adaptation retrain.}
    \label{tab:regression-cond}
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{Retrain \#1} & \textbf{Retrain \#2} \\
        \midrule
        Training macro-\(F_1\) & 0.996 & 0.981 \\
        Skin-conditions per-image failures (out of 11) & 7 & 3 \\

The two population-level tests that started green in retrain \#2
were both red in retrain \#1. They are the tests that catch
class-collapse without needing per-image labels, and they are what
told us the trade was worth making even at the cost of the
training-set metric.

\subsection{Why this is defensible}

Repurposing data across sub-models within the same pipeline is a
recognised technique --- variants of it appear in the literature
under names such as domain adaptation via auxiliary data and
hard-negative mining from adjacent tasks. It trades a small,
uniform amount of label noise for a large gain in photographic
diversity. The alternative --- sourcing several thousand
hand-curated clean-face photographs from scratch --- would have
taken days of work and produced a smaller distribution shift.

We made this trade explicit in three places: in code (the file
prefix makes the mixture auditable), in tests (the
regression suite explicitly compares before and after), and in
this book, so that any future contributor can revisit the trade
with full context.

\section{Confidence and uncertainty}\label{sec:confidence}

Every prediction carries a confidence signal. For the single-label
models it is the maximum softmax probability. For the multi-label
model it is a per-class sigmoid probability. Downstream code
treats confidence as a first-class citizen:

\begin{itemize}
    \item If either the acne or the skin-type confidence is below
        the configured floor (\texttt{confidence\_threshold}, currently
        \(0.30\)), the corresponding section of the report is
        replaced with a neutral ``low confidence'' message rather
        than the top-1 label. This is what our truthfulness rule
        looks like at runtime.
    \item For the multi-label skin-conditions model, we simply do
        not emit a finding for a class whose sigmoid probability is
        below the threshold. If no class fires, the report reads
        ``no notable conditions'' and no ingredient rules are
        triggered for that section.
\end{itemize}

The confidence threshold of \(0.30\) is deliberately low; we would
rather show a confident label with a hedged confidence bar than
hide a plausible answer entirely. Every threshold in the pipeline
is a JSON configuration entry rather than a hard-coded constant,
because we edit them often when the trade between coverage and
truthfulness shifts.

\section{The BLP recommendation engine}\label{sec:blp-engine}

The BLP engine takes a structured prediction and evaluates a
JSON-configured rule set. Each rule has a precondition (a partial
specification of the prediction), a set of ingredients to
recommend, and a plain-English explanation string that is shown to
the user.

The engine currently loads three rule maps from
\texttt{backend/blp/rules.json}: one for acne severity, one for
skin type, and one for the multi-label skin conditions. Rules are
matched in a deterministic order; matching rules union their
recommendations; the resulting ingredient list is deduplicated by
ingredient name.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,caption={A representative BLP rule, from \texttt{rules.json}.}]
"moderate": {
  "explanation": "Moderate acne detected -- multiple visible blemishes or inflamed spots.",
  "recommendations": [
    {"ingredient": "Benzoyl Peroxide (2.5-5%)",
     "reason":     "Kills acne-causing bacteria and reduces inflammation",
     "category":   "treatment"},
    {"ingredient": "Retinol (0.3-0.5%)",
     "reason":     "Increases cell turnover and prevents clogged pores",
     "category":   "treatment"},
    {"ingredient": "Niacinamide (10%)",
     "reason":     "Calms inflammation and strengthens skin barrier",
     "category":   "serum"}
  ],
  "educational_note": "Moderate acne benefits from a targeted routine. Introduce active ingredients one at a time."
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Rule design principles}

We authored the rules under three self-imposed constraints:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Every rule is defensible.} Every ingredient
        recommendation is supported by consumer-facing dermatology
        literature (peer-reviewed sources where possible, reputable
        dermatology textbooks otherwise).
    \item \textbf{No conflicting recommendations.} If two rules
        match the same prediction, they may not recommend an
        ingredient that another matching rule warns against. The
        BLP test suite includes a lint that fails if this invariant
        is ever violated.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty short-circuits recommendations.}
        When the confidence gate fires for a model, the
        corresponding section of the report is filled with a
        neutral message rather than a rule-based recommendation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Coverage over the label spaces}

The rules file must cover the label spaces we care about. Acne
severity and skin type are 4-class softmaxes, so each has four
entries. The multi-label skin conditions have three interesting
cases: pores only, blackheads only, and both simultaneously.

\chapter{Experiments}\label{ch:experiments}
%==============================================================================

This chapter reports the experiments that shaped the final system.
The largest and most important experiments --- the Phase-2
architecture comparison and the Phase-3 domain-adaptation retrain
--- have already been covered in \Cref{ch:algorithm}; this chapter
groups the remaining single-purpose experiments and the failures
that did not fit anywhere else.

\section{Experimental setup}

Unless stated otherwise, every experiment uses:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \effnet backbone with a small custom head (dropout
        \(p=0.3\), then a single linear layer).
    \item \(224 \times 224\) input, ImageNet normalisation.
    \item AdamW, weight decay \num{1e-4}, cosine learning-rate
        schedule with a short warm-up.
    \item Two-stage transfer learning as described in
        \Cref{sec:two-phase}.
    \item Early stopping on validation loss (or macro-\(F_1\) for
        multi-label) with patience 5.
\end{itemize}

A full retrain of all three models takes on the order of a few
hours on the machines we develop on.

\section{Failed experiment: naive resizing without face crop}

Very early we tried skipping face detection and letting a resized
full image go straight into the model, on the assumption that a
convolutional network would find the face by itself.

\emph{What went wrong.} Accuracy on the acne task dropped
noticeably, and confusion-matrix inspection showed the model
keying on background cues (hair, clothing, walls) that happened to
correlate with class labels in the training folder. Skin-type
accuracy was worse still, because skin type depends on skin
texture and colour, which get diluted by the surrounding pixels.

\emph{Lesson.} Face detection is not just an optimisation but a
correctness requirement: without it, the model is free to key on
whatever spurious signal correlates with the label. We
subsequently relearned this lesson the hard way in Phase 3, when
the face-crop preprocessor turned out to be present in the code
but disconnected from the runtime path (\Cref{sec:preprocessing}).

\section{Failed experiment: unweighted cross-entropy}

Skipping class weights during training saved a few lines of code.

\emph{What went wrong.} The acne classifier converged to a strong
\emph{mild}-everywhere detector. Overall accuracy looked fine on
the (imbalanced) test set, but per-class recall on \emph{severe}
was near zero. This is exactly the failure mode our truthfulness
rule forbids: a user with severe acne would be told they had mild
acne.

\emph{Lesson.} Weighted cross-entropy (\Cref{eq:weights}) is not
optional on imbalanced skin datasets. Every subsequent single-label
experiment uses it, with weights recomputed automatically from the
current split.

\section{Failed experiment: aggressive augmentation}

We tried a heavier augmentation stack borrowed from ImageNet-style
recipes: RandAugment, random erasing, strong colour jitter.

\emph{What went wrong.} Skin type is signalled largely by colour
and texture. Aggressive colour jitter destroys the signal we are
trying to classify. Random erasing removes exactly the small
regions (a patch of oily forehead, a cluster of pores) that carry
the label.

\emph{Lesson.} Augmentation must be chosen with the task in mind.
Recipes that work for object recognition do not automatically
transfer to skin classification. The final augmentation stack is
listed in \Cref{ch:data} and is deliberately gentle.

\section{The two-stage training recipe}

The two-stage transfer-learning recipe described in
\Cref{sec:two-phase} --- head-only warm-up followed by an unfreeze
of the upper backbone blocks --- was not the result of an ablation.
We adopted it from the very first day we started training an
\effnet model in Phase 2, and it is the recipe that produced the
Phase-2 numbers reported in \Cref{sec:phase2-arch}. No other
schedule was ever used for the \effnet model, so we did not have a
one-stage baseline to compare against.

We still consider the recipe a good default rather than a happy
accident. The head-only warm-up prevents the freshly-initialised
classifier head from pushing large, uninformed gradients back
through the pretrained backbone before it has learned to project
ImageNet features onto our label space; the follow-up fine-tune
adapts the upper blocks to skin-specific features while leaving
the lower, more generic layers alone. The three-way combination of
frozen lower blocks, unfrozen upper blocks, and different learning
rates for the backbone and head is what does the actual work.

\section{Backbone selection revisited}

The winning backbone was chosen empirically in the Phase-2 bake-off
(\Cref{sec:phase2-arch}). \Cref{tab:backbones} shows why the choice
also makes sense on paper: at \(224\times 224\) input, \effnet sits
in the sweet spot of parameters and FLOPs for the compute we had
to work with. ResNet-50 (a heavier ResNet variant than what we tested
in Phase 2) has roughly ten times the FLOPs of \effnet, which would
have pushed the total request latency well past our budget once
three models had to run per request. MobileNetV3-Small would fit
the budget with room to spare but its ImageNet top-1 accuracy is
several points below \effnet, so we would have been paying for
extra headroom we did not need.

\section{The real-world regression suite}\label{sec:regression}

The single largest change to our engineering practice in Phase 3
was the introduction of a real-world regression suite. The suite
lives in \texttt{tests/test\_real\_world\_regression.py} and has
three layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Per-image assertions.} Hand-labelled ground truth
        for the images in \texttt{image tests people/} is stored in
        \texttt{tests/fixtures/real\_world\_labels.json}. Each
        assertion runs the full inference pipeline (including face
        detection) on one image and checks the predicted label
        against the ground truth. Fields left as \texttt{null}
        skip cleanly, so partial labelling is fine.
    \item \textbf{Population-level sanity checks.} These check
        properties of the model's behaviour across the entire
        real-world folder without needing per-image labels. Two
        representative examples:
        \emph{``on a set of predominantly clean faces, the acne
        model MUST predict `clear' at least once''}, and
        \emph{``the multi-label skin-conditions model MUST return an
        empty findings set at least once''}. Both of these tests
        were red under the initial Phase-3 models and green under
        the final Phase-3 models. That flip is a large part of what
        we mean when we say the Phase-3 rework was necessary.
    \item \textbf{Multi-label specific assertions.} Threshold
        checks, empty-set checks, and per-class calibration checks
        added when we moved to the sigmoid architecture.
\end{enumerate}

The lesson from this suite is not that our old
\texttt{test\_model\_accuracy.py} was wrong --- it was correct at
what it did. It is that \emph{a test that pulls samples from the
training folders can only prove the model memorised its training
folders}. Real production quality has to be measured against a
distribution the model has never seen. The regression suite is our
version of that measurement.

\section{Interpreting the numbers}\label{sec:interpreting-numbers}

The three numbers in \Cref{tab:final-numbers} are best read side by
side with the class structures they measure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acne severity (72.9\,\%).} Four classes on an
        ordinal scale (\emph{clear} \(\to\) \emph{severe}). Most
        errors are between adjacent classes, which is the expected
        pattern for an ordinal task and much less harmful than a
        cross-class error. Trained on the cleanest of our datasets.
    \item \textbf{Skin type (70.9\,\%).} Four classes with genuinely
        fuzzy boundaries, particularly \emph{combination} against
        \emph{oily} and \emph{normal} against \emph{dry}. On our
        own inspection of the training data, two team members
        looking at the same photograph frequently disagreed on the
        label, which suggests the accuracy ceiling on this dataset
        is not far above where we already sit.
    \item \textbf{Skin conditions (macro-\(F_1\) \(= 0.981\)).}
        Multi-label with independent sigmoid outputs; the metric
        rewards correctly saying ``present'' or ``absent'' on each
        of the two classes independently. The number matters less
        than the qualitative change from the initial Phase-3
        softmax skin-issues model: the multi-label version can
        output the empty set on a clean face, which the softmax
        model could not.
\end{itemize}

\section{Qualitative behaviour on real inputs}

Aggregate numbers hide the texture of what the system actually
does. During Phase-3 real-world testing we settled into a small
set of patterns that recur across the hand-labelled folder:

\begin{itemize}
    \item On a well-lit, correctly-framed adult selfie the three
        cards typically agree with what a human observer would
        say. Ingredient recommendations are stable across small
        perturbations of the same face (different day, different
        lighting), which is the calibration property we care
        about.
    \item On a clean adult face the multi-label skin-conditions
        card produces the empty findings set. This is the
        behaviour the initial Phase-3 softmax skin-issues model
        could not produce; it is exercised as a hard assertion in
        the regression suite (\Cref{sec:regression}) so that it
        does not silently regress.
    \item On an under-exposed or blurry photograph the acne and
        skin-type cards typically fall below the confidence gate
        (0.30) and are replaced with the neutral ``low
        confidence'' banner. The BLP engine emits no
        recommendations for those sections. This is the
        truthfulness rule in action: rather than guess, the
        product asks the user for a better photograph.
    \item On an image with no detectable face --- either because
        no face is present or because Haar and all rotation
        fallbacks fail --- the analysis service short-circuits
        with a \texttt{NO\_FACE} status, and the frontend renders
        an actionable ``no face detected'' message rather than a
        fabricated report.
\end{itemize}

None of these patterns is exceptional; the point is that they
recur, and that they recur \emph{by design} rather than by
accident. Each of the four cases above is covered by at least one
test in \texttt{test\_real\_world\_regression.py} or
\texttt{test\_e2e\_pipeline.py}.

\section{Latency and resource profile}

Latency matters for a web application. Timing a typical
\texttt{/api/analyze} request on the machine we used for
integration testing (warm cache, three models loaded once at
startup), the total wall-clock lands in the region of roughly one
to two seconds. Each \effnet forward pass dominates its part of
the request; the three models today run sequentially, so the CNN
cost is approximately three times a single forward pass.
Everything else (image decoding, face detection, resize + ImageNet
normalisation, BLP evaluation, report persistence) is small
compared to the CNN cost.

Two consequences follow. First, if latency ever becomes a problem
the natural knob is to run the three model calls in parallel
across threads or processes; the pipeline is trivially embarrassingly
parallel at that stage. Second, moving to a lighter backbone would
buy back at most a few hundred milliseconds while giving up
several points of accuracy, which is not a trade we would take
today.

\section{Frontend}

The frontend is a React 18 single-page application. There are six
pages: \emph{Home} (landing page and disclaimer), \emph{Login} /
\emph{Register}, \emph{Analyze} (upload and camera capture),
\emph{Results} (the report), and \emph{History} (list of past
reports for the current user). Styling is mobile-first because
most casual selfies come from phones.

We deliberately kept the frontend thin. It knows how to upload an
image, poll for results, and render JSON. All logic --- including
confidence bands and colour coding --- is driven by fields returned
in the response payload. This makes it possible to iterate on the
model side without redeploying the frontend.

\section{Backend}

The backend is a FastAPI application. FastAPI was chosen for three
reasons: automatic OpenAPI schema generation (which makes the
frontend integration typed and testable), Pydantic-based request
validation (which rejects malformed inputs cheaply), and native
async support.

The main analysis endpoint is:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,caption={The main analysis endpoint (simplified).}]
@router.post("/analyze", response_model=AnalyzeResponse)
async def analyze(image: UploadFile = File(...),
                  user: User = Depends(current_user)) -> AnalyzeResponse:
    image_bytes = await image.read()
    report = await analysis_service.analyze(image_bytes, user_id=user.id)
    return AnalyzeResponse(report=report)
\end{lstlisting}

Authentication endpoints (\texttt{/auth/register},
\texttt{/auth/login}) and read endpoints (\texttt{/report/\{id\}},
\texttt{/reports}) sit next to it. Every read endpoint is scoped
to the calling user's ID.

\section{Storage}

Reports are persisted as JSON files under a \texttt{storage/}
directory. This is a deliberately boring choice: it keeps the demo
runnable without any external database and it makes reports easy
to inspect during debugging. Behind an abstract
\texttt{StorageRepository} interface, we ship a second
implementation for MongoDB (via Motor) that can be switched on
with an environment variable. We validated the Mongo path in a
dev container but the deployed demo uses JSON.

\section{Deployment}

The project ships with a \texttt{docker-compose.yml} that starts
three containers: the FastAPI backend, the nginx-fronted React
app, and a small nginx reverse proxy. Model weights are baked into
the backend image at build time so that a container start does not
depend on network access.

\chapter{Software Engineering, Testing, and MLOps}\label{ch:engineering}
%==============================================================================

A deep-learning project book usually devotes ninety per cent of its
attention to the models. We resist that convention because, in the
day-to-day life of \cara, the engineering scaffolding around the
models mattered as much as the models themselves. This chapter
documents the practice that let a small team iterate without
breaking each other's work --- and, more importantly, without
silently degrading model quality.

\section{Repository layout}

The repository is organised as a monorepo containing four
cooperating subsystems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{backend/} --- the FastAPI application, including
        the analysis service, the BLP engine, the storage
        repository, the report builder, and authentication.
    \item \texttt{model\_service/} --- everything related to the
        models: preprocessing pipeline, inference orchestrator,
        training script, and the checkpoint folder (which is
        git-ignored to keep the repository small).
    \item \texttt{frontend/} --- the React SPA and its build
        artefacts.
    \item \texttt{shared/} --- Pydantic schemas and configuration
        that is imported from both the backend and the model
        service. Keeping the schemas in a single place prevents
        drift between frontend expectations and backend responses.
\end{itemize}

Tests live in a top-level \texttt{tests/} folder rather than being
scattered next to the code they exercise. This is a deliberate
choice: it makes it easy to run the full suite as a gate, and it
discourages the anti-pattern of ``I'll write the test later''.

The test suite has 65 tests distributed across six files, as
summarised in \Cref{tab:tests}. Every file has a specific job.

\subsection{Unit tests}
The BLP tests are the fastest and the strictest. They lock down
the behaviour of the rule engine on a matrix of representative
inputs. Every time we edit the rules file, we also touch these
tests. If a rule change accidentally silences a recommendation
that used to fire, we find out within seconds.

\subsection{Integration tests}
Integration tests stub out the models with deterministic fake
predictors and exercise the report builder, the storage layer, and
the API contract. They run in a fraction of a second and catch
schema-drift bugs before they reach the frontend.

\subsection{Model accuracy tests}
The model accuracy tests load specific labelled images from a
small ground-truth folder inside the training set and assert that
the corresponding model predicts the correct top-1 label. They
form an in-distribution regression suite: a subtle bug that
degrades accuracy on the training distribution will fail one of
these tests. As Phase 3 taught us, they are not sufficient on
their own --- but they are a good, fast first line of defence.

\subsection{End-to-end tests}
The end-to-end tests exercise the full pipeline: real photograph
in, real report out. They also assert on the shape of the
recommendation set, not just the model output, so they double as
regression tests for the BLP engine.

\subsection{Real-world regression tests}
The regression tests are the piece we describe in detail in
\Cref{sec:regression}. They are the specific tests that were red
against the initial Phase-3 models and turned green one by one as
each Phase-3 fix landed. Anyone merging a change to the model
layer is expected to run them and paste the pass/fail summary in
the pull request.

\section{Debugging techniques we relied on}

Three debugging patterns paid for themselves many times over.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tensor shape assertions everywhere.} Every
        function that receives a tensor asserts its expected shape
        on entry. Silent shape bugs are the most vicious class of
        deep-learning bug; a two-line assertion turns them into
        loud failures.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic fake models in tests.} The
        \texttt{ModelRegistry} accepts a fake model in tests. That
        lets us run the entire pipeline without the CNNs, which
        speeds up the loop and pinpoints regressions in the
        plumbing rather than in the models.
    \item \textbf{Storage as a debugging surface.} Because every
        report is a JSON file, reproducing a user report is a
        one-liner: load the JSON, feed the image back through the
        pipeline, compare. We used this to diagnose more than one
        regression in the wild.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Configuration and secrets}

Runtime configuration lives in \texttt{shared/config.py} (a
\texttt{pydantic\_settings} model) and is populated from
environment variables via \texttt{.env}. Sensitive values ---
JWT secret, database credentials --- are provided by the
environment, never committed to the repository. Every configuration
knob is a knob a future maintainer must understand, so we keep the
list short.

\section{Checkpoint management}

Training saves \emph{only} the best checkpoint by the validation
metric (accuracy for single-label, macro-\(F_1\) for multi-label).
There is no ``last'' checkpoint. This is a deliberate choice: it
prevents the temptation to ship the last-epoch model, which is
almost always slightly worse than the best-val-metric model but
looks tempting because it is the last one on disk. Checkpoints
live under \texttt{model\_service/checkpoints/} and are baked into
the backend Docker image at build time.

The training script also supports a \texttt{--resume} mode. That
mode was introduced in Phase 3 so we could refine an existing
checkpoint without paying the cost of a full retrain when we
tweaked the negatives folder, augmentations, or the multi-label
threshold. Iteration speed on Phase-3 experiments improved
noticeably as a result.

\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\section{Answers to the research questions}

We opened the book with a short list of empirical questions. It
is worth revisiting each of them with the evidence collected in
the intervening chapters.

\begin{description}
    \item[Which backbone family generalises best on our data budget?]
        On a fair Phase-2 bake-off with ImageNet-pretrained
        initialisations on ACNE04, the ranking was clear: ResNet
        \(\sim 55\,\%\), DenseNet \(\sim 62\,\%\), ViT
        \(\sim 68\,\%\), \effnet \(73.1\,\%\). The most
        credible explanation is not that \effnet is a smarter
        architecture than a Vision Transformer, but that at our
        dataset scale the strength of the pretrained weights
        dominates: compound-scaled CNNs with a mature pretraining
        recipe simply arrive at good features faster. On more data
        or more compute the ranking might close, but on the
        hardware we had \effnet was the correct pick.
    \item[Independent classifiers vs shared-backbone multi-task.]
        On our data budget the independent-classifier design won.
        A shared backbone would have been pulled around by
        whichever head had the loudest gradient at any moment,
        making it very hard to attribute a real-world failure to
        a specific sub-task. Splitting into three separate models
        cost us a small amount of memory and latency, both
        comfortably inside the budget, and gave us three cleanly
        interpretable predictions.
    \item[Softmax vs sigmoid + BCE for skin conditions.] The
        reformulation was the single largest behavioural change
        in Phase 3. Under softmax the skin-issues model
        hallucinated at least one condition on every clean face;
        under sigmoid + BCE, with a properly constructed negative
        folder, the model can output the empty set, which is what
        a clean face should trigger. Two population-level
        regression tests flipped from red to green because of
        this change alone.
    \item[Do validation-split metrics predict shipped quality?]
        No. Our initial Phase-3 skin-issues model scored around
        98\,\% validation accuracy and could not predict
        \texttt{healthy} on any real photograph. The follow-up
        skin-conditions retrain \#1 scored a macro-\(F_1\) of
        \(0.996\) and misfired \texttt{blackheads} on 7 of 11
        real photographs. In both cases the validation number was
        misleading in the same way: the test split shared the
        training split's photographic style, so the model was
        rewarded for memorising style rather than feature. The
        regression suite --- per-image plus population-level, on
        a distribution matched to production --- is the only
        measure we now trust before shipping.
    \item[Cross-task data reuse.] Yes, within limits. Adding
        1{,}448 skin-type photographs as negatives for the
        skin-conditions model closed most of the domain gap
        without a fresh labelling effort. The visible cost was
        \(0.015\) of training macro-\(F_1\); the visible gain
        was the flip from 7 to 3 real-world failures out of 11
        and the two population-level tests turning green. The
        label noise from mis-selected negatives is real but
        small.
\end{description}

\section{What the Phase-3 rework actually cost}

None of the Phase-3 fixes involved a bigger backbone, a fancier
optimiser, or a longer training run. The wins came from four
places: (i) matching preprocessing at inference and training,
(ii) rewriting one task as multi-label, (iii) reusing data across
sub-models, and (iv) building a regression suite that measured
the right distribution. In aggregate these interventions took
substantially less compute than the initial Phase-2 training runs
and produced a much better product. The general lesson is that on
small, noisy datasets, data quality and evaluation design beat
model complexity nearly every time.

\section{Threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset shift.} Our datasets skew toward young
        adults and lighter Fitzpatrick skin types. Real users
        will be more diverse. The regression suite already
        surfaces this when we add a photograph the models are
        not calibrated for; the honest response is to acknowledge
        the limitation in the disclaimer rather than pretend it
        does not exist.
    \item \textbf{Label noise.} Even the anchor dataset (ACNE04)
        has label disagreements between raters. Our accuracy
        numbers are, at best, upper bounds on ``agreement with
        the original labelling'', not on ``agreement with a
        dermatologist''.
    \item \textbf{Multi-label negatives.} The 1{,}448 skin-type
        photographs used as auxiliary negatives contain some
        genuine mild pores or blackheads that we now treat as
        \([0, 0]\). This is unavoidable label noise; empirically
        it is small enough that generalisation improves, but it
        is not zero.
    \item \textbf{Confidence calibration.} We do not currently
        temperature-scale the softmax outputs. The confidence
        gate at threshold \(0.30\) is deliberately generous.
        Users should treat a confidence bar in the ``high''
        range as ``very likely'', not ``certain''.
    \item \textbf{Small regression set.} The real-world set is
        11 photographs. It is enough to catch the specific
        Phase-2 bugs and to detect a class-collapse failure, but
        it is not enough to make fine-grained claims about
        subgroup fairness. Extending it is high on the
        future-work list (\Cref{ch:future}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethical considerations}

An automated skin analyser sits close to sensitive territory.
We mitigate the main risks in three ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The disclaimer on the landing page is unambiguous:
        \cara is not a medical diagnostic tool.
    \item The system refuses to render a confident label when the
        model is not confident, precisely to avoid giving false
        assurance about a skin condition that might warrant
        professional attention.
    \item No user photograph is retained beyond the report unless
        the user explicitly requests to keep it. Reports
        themselves contain no personally identifying information
        beyond the user's own account ID.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\chapter{Conclusions}\label{ch:conclusions}
%==============================================================================

We set out to build a web application that would deliver a
truthful, useful, and explainable report on a user's facial skin
from a single photograph, and we shipped one. The final system
--- three independent \effnet classifiers behind a face-aware
preprocessing pipeline, a multi-label skin-conditions model
trained with cross-task negative anchors, and a deterministic
rule engine on top --- is the product of three phases of work in
which the largest single accelerator was almost never a new
architecture. It was data quality, task framing, and evaluation
design.

The lessons we would carry into a next project:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Freeze the label spaces and audit the folders before
        touching a training script. A mislabelled folder that
        looks fine on a validation split is nearly invisible
        until it hits real inputs.
    \item Match preprocessing at training and inference from day
        one. Dead code that claims to be shared is worse than no
        code.
    \item Pick the head shape (softmax vs sigmoid) from the
        physics of the problem, not from habit. If the world
        allows ``none'' or ``multiple'', BCE + sigmoid is the
        honest choice.
    \item Build a regression suite before you build a model. The
        cost of doing so is one afternoon; the cost of not doing
        so is a full rework.
    \item Truthfulness is a design constraint that cuts across
        every layer of the stack. The backend has to know how to
        say ``I do not know''; the frontend has to know how to
        render it; the rule engine has to know when to stop
        firing rules. Any layer that hides uncertainty
        invalidates the promise the other layers try to keep.
\end{itemize}

\cara is not the largest or the most accurate skin analyser in the
world. Within the constraints we set ourselves --- a modest compute
budget, no dermatologist on the team, an insistence that every
recommendation be traceable --- it is a system we are willing to
stand behind.

%==============================================================================
\chapter{Future Work}\label{ch:future}
%==============================================================================

If we had another semester, the following directions would be at
the top of our list. They are ordered by expected impact per hour
of effort.

\section{Better data for the minority classes}

The \emph{severe} acne class and the \emph{combination} skin-type
class are both under-represented in our datasets. Curating a
couple of hundred additional images per class --- ideally with
dermatologist labels --- would probably lift the acne and skin-type
accuracies more than any architectural change.

\section{Ordinal regression for acne severity}

Acne severity is inherently ordinal. Treating it as a categorical
classification wastes information and produces most of the confusion
between adjacent classes. Reformulating it as ordinal regression, or
as label-distribution learning as in \cite{wu2019acne04}, is a
promising path.

\section{Attention-based visualisation}

Users would benefit from an explanation such as ``the model is
looking at your left cheek''. A Grad-CAM overlay or attention map
would be inexpensive to add and would substantially strengthen
the truthfulness story.

\section{On-device inference}

Running three \effnet models sequentially is fast enough on a
desktop or laptop but not always fast enough on a low-end phone.
Converting the models to ONNX and then to a mobile runtime (e.g.\
ONNX Runtime Mobile or Core ML) is a natural next step.

\section{Larger regression set with diverse skin tones}

Our real-world regression set is small (11 photographs). Extending
it to a few hundred, deliberately balanced across Fitzpatrick
skin tones, would let us measure fairness properly and would
almost certainly reveal new failure modes to fix.

\section{Formal dermatologist review of the rules}

Our BLP rules are grounded in publicly available skincare
knowledge, but they have not been reviewed by a dermatologist as a
coherent product. A formal review would let us sharpen the rules
file and would open the door to eventual regulatory review if the
project ever became commercial.

\chapter{Glossary}

\begin{description}
    \item[BCE] Binary Cross-Entropy loss --- the loss function of a
        multi-label classifier with sigmoid outputs.
    \item[BLP] Business Logic Processing --- our internal name for
        the rule engine that converts model predictions into
        recommendations.
    \item[EfficientNet] A family of convolutional backbones that
        jointly scale depth, width, and input resolution. We use
        the smallest member, EfficientNet-B0.
    \item[EXIF orientation] Metadata attached by phone cameras
        recording how the sensor was oriented when the shot was
        taken. Ignored by \texttt{cv2.imdecode}, respected by
        PIL's \texttt{ImageOps.exif\_transpose}. See
        \Cref{sec:rotation}.
    \item[Grad-CAM] Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping ---
        a technique for visualising which pixels contributed to a
        prediction. Referenced in Future Work.
    \item[Haar cascade] The classical OpenCV object detector.
        Fast, upright-frontal only. We supplement it with a
        rotation retry.
    \item[Macro-\(F_1\)] The unweighted mean of per-class \(F_1\)
        scores. The default metric for our multi-label
        skin-conditions model.
    \item[MediaPipe] A Google library for real-time perception
        tasks including face detection. Considered as an
        alternative to Haar.
    \item[Multi-label classification] The setting in which the
        model may output any subset of \(K\) classes for a given
        input, including the empty set. Implemented with
        independent sigmoids and BCE loss.
    \item[ONNX] Open Neural Network Exchange --- a portable
        interchange format for trained models. Referenced in
        Future Work.
    \item[Softmax] The final normalisation function of a
        classification head; converts logits to a probability
        distribution over mutually exclusive classes.
    \item[Transfer learning] Initialising a network with weights
        pretrained on a large dataset (ImageNet in our case) and
        fine-tuning it on the target task.
    \item[Two-stage training] Our recipe: train the head with the
        backbone frozen, then unfreeze the upper backbone blocks
        with a lower learning rate.
    \item[Uncertain (report field)] The state in which the
        frontend replaces a predicted label with a neutral message
        because the model's confidence was below the per-model
        floor.
\end{description}
